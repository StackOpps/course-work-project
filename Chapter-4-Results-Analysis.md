Chapter 4: Results and Analysis

4.1 Overview

This chapter evaluates the implementation described in Chapter 3 against the criteria set out in Section 3.4, using the evidence the system itself has produced: the drill history recorded in `docs/data/history.json`, the CI/CD workflow definitions that generated it, and the Terraform configuration that defines what is actually running. Where the evidence is thin, that is stated plainly rather than papered over. This revision reflects a second recorded drill, which changes the picture materially for some criteria and leaves others exactly as they were.

At the time of writing, two full DR failover drills have been recorded through `dr_failover.yml` — one on 2026-08-07 and one on 2026-08-08 — and no results from AWS Backup's own restore-testing plan have yet been recorded by `record_restore_testing.yml`; both entries in the history file to date are failover drills, not independent restore-testing-plan runs. Cost data collection via AWS Cost Explorer (`record_cost.yml`) remains wired into the pipeline but had not yet produced a recorded snapshot at the time of writing. The analysis below works with what actually exists rather than projecting forward, and each section is read against the criteria and measurement methods defined in Section 3.4.

4.2 Recovery Time Objective

The first recorded drill (triggered 2026-08-07T13:12:47Z against `shop.crafthavens.com`) produced an RTO of 16.29 seconds — the time from the workflow's recorded start to the Route 53 cutover. As set out in the previous revision of this chapter, that figure was treated with suspicion rather than reported as a success: an RDS restore from a snapshot routinely takes several minutes even for a small instance class, well before EC2, the ALB, or DNS come into play, so a genuine cold-start recovery could not plausibly complete end-to-end in 16 seconds. The likely explanation offered was that the DR region already held matching infrastructure when that drill ran, so `terraform apply` converged almost instantly because there was little left to create.

The second drill (triggered 2026-08-08T12:37:47Z, completed 2026-08-08T12:48:59Z) produced an RTO of 672.53 seconds — 11 minutes and 13 seconds. This is roughly forty times larger than the first result, and the order of magnitude is far more consistent with a genuine rebuild: an RDS snapshot restore, EC2 and ALB provisioning, a smoke check, and a Route 53 cutover chained together plausibly take single-digit to low double-digit minutes, not seconds. Read alongside the first result, this is the direction the earlier analysis anticipated — a drill that behaves like an actual reconstruction rather than a near-instant convergence against infrastructure that was already there.

That said, this should not be overstated. The second drill was not run as a deliberately controlled cold-start test in the sense Section 5.1 calls for — there is no confirmation in the workflow's own output that the DR region's compute state was empty immediately beforehand, only that the resulting time is far more plausible than the first. With two data points showing an order-of-magnitude difference, it is defensible to say the evidence no longer points cleanly toward the 16-second figure being representative, but it is not yet defensible to name 11 minutes 13 seconds as a validated worst-case RTO. That distinction matters more than either number in isolation: the mechanism itself continues to run the correct sequence and produce a value automatically, but a trustworthy worst-case figure still requires the controlled methodology set out in Section 5.1, run enough times to distinguish a genuine floor from an artefact of whatever state the DR region happened to be in on a given attempt.

4.3 Recovery Point Objective

The first drill recorded an RPO of 2,746 seconds (45 minutes 46 seconds); the second recorded an RPO of 532 seconds (8 minutes 52 seconds). Both are measured the same way — the age of the RDS recovery point actually restored from, relative to the drill's start time — and both are more trustworthy than either RTO figure, because this measurement is not sensitive to whatever state the DR region happened to be in; it comes directly from the timestamp AWS Backup attached to the recovery point at creation.

Both values sit inside the range the design should produce. The backup plan runs hourly with a 180-second completion window (Section 3.2), so the theoretical worst case for RPO is a little over 63 minutes — the gap between one hourly backup finishing just after a failure and the next one not yet having started. 45 minutes 46 seconds and 8 minutes 52 seconds both fall comfortably inside that bound, and between them now span a considerably wider slice of the hourly cycle than a single sample could: one landed well into the hour, the other close to its start. That two independently timed drills, taken roughly a day apart, both respected the bound is modest but genuine evidence that the hourly schedule behaves as designed across different points in its cycle, rather than the first result having simply landed in a favourable spot.

The limitation from the previous revision still applies, only slightly reduced: two measurements are enough to show the bound holding at two different phases of the cycle, but not enough to characterise the distribution of RPO values a real user would experience. A meaningful RPO claim still needs drills spread systematically across the backup cycle, as Section 5.1 sets out.

4.4 Restore Success Rate

Combining the two full failover drills with AWS Backup's own restore-testing plan gives a restore success rate of 100% (2 of 2) at the time of writing — the dashboard computes this live from `history.json` rather than it being asserted here. Both successes to date come from manually dispatched failover drills; no run of `record_restore_testing.yml` has yet contributed an independent restore-testing-plan result to this figure. The evidence base has doubled since the previous revision, but it is worth being just as explicit as before about what that means: two drills is still a small enough sample that "100%" and "neither attempt so far has failed" remain close to the same statement. The figure will become meaningfully more informative once AWS Backup's hourly restore-testing plan has folded in results of its own, since those accumulate automatically without a human needing to trigger anything.

4.5 Automation Level

This criterion is read from the workflow definitions rather than measured from drill data, and neither recorded drill changes that reading. `dr_failover.yml` runs Terraform re-apply, RDS restore lookup, smoke check, and DNS cutover as one unattended sequence once dispatched — the only human step is the dispatch itself, which is a deliberate decision point rather than a gap, given the system has no automated failure detection to trigger it otherwise (Section 4.9 of the previous revision, carried forward in Section 5.1, returns to this). `record_restore_testing.yml` and the AWS Backup restore-testing plan it polls run entirely on AWS-managed and repo-scheduled cron, with no human step at all. The place automation remains genuinely incomplete is upstream of recovery: provisioning and teardown both still require a workflow_dispatch click, so a small team running this in practice would need someone to notice an outage and start the drill, not just wait for a page.

4.6 Backup Protection

AWS Backup Vault Lock is enabled in Terraform for both the primary and DR vaults (`backup/vault.tf`), with a one-day minimum retention: once a recovery point is created, it cannot be deleted before that day passes, by anyone, including the account root user. The compromise that made this possible without breaking the testbed's cost-bounding teardown is architectural rather than a disabled control — the backup vaults were pulled out of the 2-hourly `destroy.yml` schedule that tears down `infra/`, since AWS Backup refuses to delete a vault holding locked recovery points and repeatedly trying to do so would just fail. The vaults are destroyed only on a manual, opt-in run of `destroy.yml`, once the retention window has actually elapsed.

The previous revision of this chapter noted that the one recorded drill predated Vault Lock being enabled, so no drill result reflected a run against a Vault-Lock-protected vault. That caveat is now partially resolved: the repository's commit history shows Vault Lock was enabled ahead of the second drill, not after it — the change landed shortly before that drill was dispatched, which means the second recorded result is the first to have run with Vault Lock live on both vaults. The first drill, from 2026-08-07, still predates the change and does not reflect it.

Two caveats from the previous revision still stand. First, a one-day minimum is a testbed-scale compromise, not what a production small-business deployment would choose — a real deployment would likely set this closer to the 7-35 day range common in industry guidance, accepting the higher storage cost of vaults that can't be torn down on a short cycle. Second, one drill running successfully under Vault Lock demonstrates that the control does not interfere with normal recovery, but it is a single data point on that question as much as anything else in this chapter is.

4.7 Monitoring and Auditability

This criterion is verified directly from the deployed configuration rather than from drill data, and neither recorded drill changes that verification. CloudWatch alarms exist in both regions for EC2 status checks, RDS free storage, and AWS Backup job/copy/restore state changes, each wired to a per-region SNS topic (`backup/monitoring.tf`, `infra/monitoring.tf`), and a single multi-region CloudTrail trail logs backup, restore, and configuration-change events. This remains a structural property of the deployed configuration rather than something a drill measures, so it stays verified regardless of how many drills have run.

4.8 Cost

Cost is pulled from AWS Cost Explorer daily by `record_cost.yml` and rendered as whole-account spend on the dashboard, rather than only being bounded through infrastructure teardown as it was at the design stage. At the time of writing this had still not produced a recorded figure, since it is a scheduled daily poll rather than something triggered by a drill, so this chapter continues to report the instrumentation rather than a specific dollar amount it has not yet measured. What can be said with confidence is that the structural cost story from Chapter 3 still holds regardless of the exact figure: the DR region carries no standing compute, only a backup vault, and `infra/` (the EC2/RDS/ALB tier that actually costs money) is torn down on a recurring schedule between sessions. Once Cost Explorer produces its first recorded snapshot, that bound can be stated as a measured number rather than an assumption.

4.9 Infrastructure Reproducibility

This criterion did not have a dedicated section in the previous revision of this chapter, despite being one of the criteria set out in Section 3.4; it is added here to close that gap. It is verified structurally rather than measured from a single metric: the application stack is destroyed and re-provisioned from Terraform on a recurring schedule in the primary region, and every recovery drill re-applies that same code against the DR region rather than following a separate, hand-maintained recovery procedure. With two recorded drills now on file, this is no longer a claim resting on the design alone — the same Terraform configuration has been applied against the DR region twice, on different days, and both applications completed successfully as part of a drill that went on to pass its smoke check and DNS cutover. That is a small but genuine confirmation that the same code path provisions a working environment in the second region repeatably, not just once.

4.10 Discussion

Read together, these results support a narrower claim than "the system meets its design goals," and a more useful one: the automation pipeline works end-to-end and repeatably, and the measurement most resistant to environmental noise (RPO) is consistent with the hourly backup design across two independent attempts. The RTO picture has shifted meaningfully between the two drills — from a figure that could not plausibly represent a genuine recovery to one that is at least the right order of magnitude for one — without yet reaching the point of being a validated worst-case number. That is a fixable methodological gap, not a flaw in the recovery mechanism itself, and Section 5.1 sets out what a drill needs to control for to produce an RTO figure worth citing on its own.

The design's core trade-off argument from Chapter 3 — cheaper and faster than manual restore, cheaper than hot standby, at the cost of a manual trigger and (previously) unenforced backup protection — is better supported now than at either earlier point in this project. Backup Protection has moved from "implemented but disabled" to "enabled and running," and the second recorded drill is now direct evidence that a real recovery completes successfully with that control live, not just that the control exists in Terraform. Infrastructure Reproducibility, evaluated explicitly for the first time in this revision, adds a second thread to the same argument: the same code that provisions the primary environment has now provisioned the DR environment successfully twice, which is exactly the kind of repeatable evidence a reproducibility claim needs and previously lacked. Taken together, the picture this chapter supports is one of an automation pipeline whose mechanics are demonstrably sound and increasingly well evidenced, with its headline recovery-speed number still awaiting the controlled measurement that would let it be reported with confidence.
